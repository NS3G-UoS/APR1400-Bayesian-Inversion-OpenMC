A proof-of-concept study with heteroscedastic Gaussian-process emulation and leave-one-out blind validation

Abstract
Reactor-physics inverse problems are usually validated on synthetic, noise-free data, leaving open the question of whether real stochastic transport calculations can recover an unknown design parameter. The authors tested this directly: an effective gadolinium-strength parameter, α_Gd, was swept across 13 real OpenMC criticality calculations for a C2-map-based APR1400 assembly, reducing k∞ by 786 pcm (SNR 8.2) despite Monte Carlo noise of order 70 pcm. A heteroscedastic Gaussian-process surrogate and a deterministic Bayesian posterior, no MCMC, were then validated by genuinely blind leave-one-out inversion of every case. Recovery achieved a posterior-mean MAE of 0.024, and a fully held-out truth (αGd = 1.075) was rediscovered from its k∞ alone to within 0.7% relative error. The results show that a single integral response can identify the direction and magnitude of an absorber-strength parameter, but not pin it down tightly, motivating a Gd-specific observable as the next step.

1. Introduction
High-fidelity neutron-transport analysis is central to modern reactor design because it preserves detailed geometry and continuous-energy nuclear data while providing statistically defensible criticality predictions. OpenMC provides an open-source Monte Carlo framework for these calculations [1], and the ENDF/B-VII.1 library supplies the evaluated reaction, covariance, fission-yield, and decay data used in the present model [2]. The APR1400 benchmark hierarchy further provides a recognized basis for assessing pin-wise and high-fidelity methods for 16 x 16 pressurized-water-reactor CEA  [3]. The present work uses the benchmark C2 loading map only as the geometric basis of a reduced assembly-level inverse problem; it does not claim to reproduce the full benchmark state or reference solution.
Bayesian calibration and inverse uncertainty quantification (IUQ) infer uncertain model parameters from observed responses rather than propagating uncertain inputs only in the forward direction. Kennedy and O’Hagan established a general Bayesian framework for calibrating computer models [4], and Rasmussen and Williams developed the Gaussian-process (GP) foundations that enable probabilistic emulation of expensive simulators [5]. In nuclear engineering, modular GP-based IUQ has emphasized model discrepancy, numerical uncertainty, and identifiability [6], while broader reviews have identified transparency and tractability as essential requirements for practical deployment [7].
The objective of this study is to determine whether a statepoint-backed, one-dimensional Bayesian workflow can recover an effective absorber-strength parameter from stochastic Monte Carlo criticality calculations on modest computing hardware. The methodology combines a heteroscedastic GP treatment of OpenMC sampling uncertainty, direct posterior quadrature, and systematic leave-one-out blind recovery using only real transport points. This design makes failure modes and identifiability visible before extension to multiple observables or calibration parameters.

        1.1 Related work and novelty of the present contribution
Recent reactor-physics literature reflects a broad and mature forward sensitivity/uncertainty (S/U) tradition. Frosio et al. extended Bayesian data adjustment and representativity theory to multiple correlated experiments and coupled Boltzmann-Bateman problems [8]. Cabellos et al. propagated covariance data for U-235, U-238, and Pu-239 to the effective multiplication factor of fast-spectrum benchmarks and showed that uncertainty estimates can depend strongly on evaluated-library covariances and reaction correlations [9]. Bidaud and El-Amami examined criticality-constrained breeding-ratio sensitivities and showed that a k-reset constraint can substantially alter the magnitudes, signs, rankings, and correlation effects of sensitivities [10]. Valocchi et al. propagated newly evaluated delayed-neutron precursor-abundance covariances to the effective delayed-neutron fraction and demonstrated the importance of covariance structure and anticorrelation [11]. Bécares and Panizo assessed multiplication-factor and reactivity-effect uncertainties for fluoride-salt reactor concepts and found strong dependence on the reactor response, spectrum, and covariance library [12]. Collectively, these studies establish that forward nuclear-data uncertainty in integral reactor responses can be substantial and configuration dependent. None, however, addresses the inverse recovery of an unknown design or manufacturing parameter from a noisy integral response, which is the focus of the present work.
The closest precedent in surrogate modeling is the OpenMC-based GP study by Molczan et al. [13]. That work demonstrated calibrated predictive uncertainty for PWR pin-cell criticality using a heteroscedastic GP trained on 400 Monte Carlo calculations. The present study uses a Matérn-family GP, stochastic OpenMC responses, and an explicit treatment of Monte Carlo uncertainty. The scientific objective is different, Molczan et al. solved the forward emulation problem whereas the present study inverts the surrogate to recover an unknown absorber-strength parameter from an observed response and validates the inference using statepoint-backed blind holdouts.
Probabilistic treatment of stochastic simulation output is itself an active research area. Diaconeasa and Mosleh incorporated multiple sampling strategies into the ADS-IDAC dynamic probabilistic safety-assessment framework to propagate epistemic and aleatory uncertainty through discrete dynamic event trees [14]. Hajas et al. developed a forced-correlation variance-reduction method for dynamic Monte Carlo neutron-population calculations using a stochastic differential equation representation of the transport process [15]. These studies reinforce that stochastic simulation error is not merely a fixed nuisance quantity: its propagation, correlation, and reduction require explicit methodological treatment. In the present work, the statepoint-derived Monte Carlo uncertainty is incorporated into the GP training model and the Bayesian likelihood rather than being ignored or replaced with noise-free synthetic data.
Positioned against this literature, the present contribution is distinguished by four elements. First, unlike forward S/U analyses [8]-[12], it solves an inverse problem: an unknown effective gadolinium-strength parameter is recovered from an integral criticality response. Second, unlike conventional train/test or random-split surrogate validation [13], it performs genuine blind parameter discovery by withholding each of the 13 OpenMC cases in turn from both GP training and likelihood construction until inference is complete. Third, the one-dimensional posterior is evaluated via direct deterministic quadrature on a 5,001-point grid rather than via Markov chain Monte Carlo, thereby eliminating the need for convergence diagnostics for a problem whose dimensionality does not justify sampling-based inference [6], [7]. Fourth, every quantity entering the analysis originates from executed OpenMC calculations, including the pointwise statistical uncertainties; no synthetic or noise-free response surface is used. These features make the workflow transparent and reproducible, and establish a baseline for evaluating whether an integral criticality response alone is sufficient to identify a burnable-absorber parameter in future fuel-design, digital-twin, surveillance, and uncertainty-informed licensing applications.

3.2 Heteroscedastic Gaussian-process emulator
A separate Gaussian-process (GP) regression model was fitted to the forward relationship . The GP is a surrogate for the OpenMC forward calculation, not an inverse model: its purpose is to provide a continuous probabilistic estimate of  at any  inside the sampled interval, together with an uncertainty associated with interpolation. The input and response were standardized before training. A constant kernel multiplied by a Matérn  kernel was used, consistent with a smooth but not infinitely differentiable one-dimensional response [5] [6]. The squared OpenMC standard deviations were supplied as point-specific observation-noise variances, so training explicitly accounts for the fact that different Monte Carlo cases have different statistical precision.

Heteroscedasticity is introduced through the known Monte Carlo uncertainty rather than through a separately learned noise process. For training point , scikit-learn receives the normalized variance . Consequently, a case with a larger OpenMC standard deviation is treated as less precise and exerts less leverage on the latent response than a case with a smaller standard deviation. This distinction is important because the scatter seen between neighboring  values is comparable to the effect of a single 0.025 parameter increment.
The GP returns two quantities at every candidate : a predictive mean , used as the surrogate forward response, and a predictive standard deviation , representing uncertainty in the interpolated latent response. The latter is carried explicitly into the Bayesian likelihood; it is not used as a replacement for OpenMC statistical uncertainty. Thus, posterior uncertainty grows when either the observation is noisy or the surrogate is weakly constrained between training points.
In every leave-one-out test, the GP was refitted after removing the case being inferred. Therefore, the surrogate used to discover a held-out  never had access to that point during training. This prevents data leakage and turns each leave-one-out calculation into a genuine extrapolation-based inverse-recovery test rather than a reconstruction of a training datum.
The GP is used only inside the sampled prior interval 0.85 ≤  ≤ 1.15; no extrapolation beyond this domain is permitted. 3.4 Leave-one-out blind validation
The supplied Phase I archive contained a manifest value  but no independent OpenMC transport calculation at that parameter. Therefore, constructing a posterior for 1.073 would have required inventing an observation and was rejected. Instead, each of the 13 real OpenMC points was treated in turn as a blind observation: the point was removed, the GP was refitted to the remaining 12 cases, and a posterior was calculated using only the held-out  value and its uncertainty. This provides 13 genuinely statepoint-backed inverse-recovery tests.

5. Discussion
5.1 What inverse results establish
The first result is positive: an integral criticality response can identify the direction and approximate magnitude of a single effective absorber parameter with very few Monte Carlo design points. The endpoint SNR of 8.2 confirms that the chosen parameter yields a response that exceeds the stochastic uncertainty over the full prior range. The GP therefore does not need to manufacture a trend from noise; it interpolates a clear transport signal. The  holdout makes this result concrete: after withholding the case entirely, the inversion recovered a posterior mean of 1.0825 and a MAP of 1.0835, corresponding to relative errors of approximately 0.69% and 0.79%, respectively.
The second result is equally important: global sensitivity does not imply strong local identifiability. A 0.025 change in  corresponds to only about 58 pcm according to the linear diagnostic slope, which is of the same order as the 61–74 pcm one-sigma OpenMC uncertainties. Consequently, neighboring parameter values are difficult to distinguish from  alone. This explains both the broad 95% credible intervals and the modest mean absolute recovery error. Increasing particle histories would improve local resolution, but a more efficient approach is to add a response more directly connected to Gd absorption.
5.2 Interpretation and adequacy of the surrogate model
The surrogate is central to the inversion because the OpenMC campaign provides only 13 discrete parameter values, whereas the posterior is evaluated on 5,001 candidate values. Without a surrogate, inference would either be restricted to the original grid or would require an arbitrary deterministic interpolation that does not quantify interpolation uncertainty. The GP converts the discrete stochastic OpenMC calculations into a continuous probability model for the forward response, while retaining uncertainty about the response between sampled points. This is precisely the role for which GP emulators are commonly used in Bayesian calibration of expensive computer models [4] [5] [6].
The fitted response should not be interpreted as evidence that the underlying physics is intrinsically Gaussian or that  is exactly a GP realization. Rather, the GP is a statistical emulator of the smooth one-dimensional map generated by OpenMC. Its suitability here follows from three empirical observations: (i) the response has a strong global trend with endpoint SNR 8.22, (ii) deviations from monotonicity are small relative to combined Monte Carlo uncertainty, and (iii) the optimized long Matérn length scale indicates that the data do not support rapid oscillatory structure. These features make a low-complexity Matérn GP a more defensible choice than a high-capacity neural surrogate for the present 13-point dataset.
The surrogate nevertheless introduces an additional source of uncertainty and possible bias. Near the center of the sampled domain, multiple neighboring OpenMC points constrain the GP, whereas uncertainty generally increases as support becomes sparse or when a point is removed during leave-one-out validation. The Bayesian likelihood therefore includes  explicitly. The broad posterior obtained for the  blind case is not a failure of the surrogate; it reflects the combined fact that adjacent OpenMC responses differ by roughly the same order as their Monte Carlo uncertainty and that  is an integral response with limited local parameter resolution. A tighter posterior requires either more precise transport data, a more informative response, or both.
A further caveat is that the present GP treats the supplied pointwise OpenMC variances as independent training-noise variances. Because the available Phase I archive reused a common Monte Carlo seed across cases, some cross-case error correlation may exist and is not represented by the diagonal noise model. This is one plausible contributor to the observed 84.6% leave-one-out coverage relative to the nominal 95% level, another key contribution is the one-sided nature of the endpoint holdouts. Future calculations with unique deterministic seeds, or an explicitly estimated covariance model, will make the probabilistic interpretation of the surrogate and posterior more rigorous.
5.3 Why a Gd-specific response is the logical next observable
A Gd-isotope absorption tally would provide a local physics observable whose sensitivity is directly attributable to the parameter being calibrated, while  would retain its role as an integral neutronic constraint. The combined posterior should therefore reduce parameter degeneracy and provide a stronger physics-based identifiability argument than  alone. This is consistent with the broader IUQ principle that calibration quality depends not only on the number of observations but also on their sensitivity and independence [6] [7].

5.5 Implications for an industry-oriented workflow
A practical inverse-calibration workflow for fuel-design or reactor-analysis applications should preserve the separation demonstrated here between data generation and statistical inference. Phase I should create an immutable, statepoint-traceable response database, while Phase II should be able to change priors, emulators, likelihoods, and diagnostics without rerunning transport. This separation improves reproducibility and supports independent QA. For a one-dimensional parameter, direct posterior quadrature is also preferable to MCMC because it is deterministic, inexpensive, and makes multimodality or boundary concentration immediately visible.
For the corrected follow-on study, the most informative comparison would be three posteriors obtained from the same blind truth:  only,  only, and the combined response. A second experiment could then vary Monte Carlo precision to quantify the trade-off between particle histories and posterior width. Only after these steps are stable would adaptively sampling or a second calibration parameter be justified.
6. Conclusions
A 13-point OpenMC sweep over  produced a clear global criticality response of 785.9 pcm with an endpoint SNR of 8.22, and a heteroscedastic Matérn GP represented this one-dimensional stochastic response while incorporating point-specific OpenMC uncertainties. Direct Bayesian grid evaluation, without MCMC, recovered leave-one-out parameter values with a posterior mean MAE of 0.0240 and an RMSE of 0.0278. In the real blind  holdout experiment, the model was trained without that case and was supplied only its  response; it recovered a posterior mean of 1.0825 and MAP of 1.0835, corresponding to relative errors of approximately 0.69% and 0.79%, respectively, while the 95% CrI [1.0119, 1.1435] contained the hidden value. The broad posterior interval nevertheless shows that  alone is not locally informative enough at the current Monte Carlo precision for tight Gd calibration. The current Phase I implementation is not benchmark-quality due to temperature, material mixing, geometry, seed, and missing tally issues, and these must be corrected before physical APR1400 interpretation. The recommended next study is a corrected C2 campaign with independent truth data and a Gd155+Gd157 absorption-to-fission response, followed by k-only, Gd-response-only, and combined Bayesian inversions.
